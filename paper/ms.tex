\author{Gaurav Sood}

\begin{abstract}
Equivalent covariate codings should give the same predictions,
treatment-effect estimates, and uncertainty under a declared family of
recodings. We formalize this finite-sample requirement, distinguishing
changes of units, origins, and signs from arbitrary monotone transformations.
In an observational job-training example, negating continuous covariates
changes an ATT targeted minimum loss-based estimate using first-order
Highly Adaptive Lasso (HAL) nuisances from $\TmleOriginal$ to
$\TmleReflected$ dollars with the sample, folds, and settings held fixed.
Both targeting calculations pass score and step-size checks, and neither
nuisance fit reports a convergence warning. Standardizing and canonically
orienting covariates removes the observed sign and unit dependence to
numerical precision, while retaining nuisance convergence warnings.
For zero-order HAL, a symmetric functional dictionary with minimum-norm
solution selection gives an affine-equivariant ideal estimator. An exact
counterexample shows why interpolated knots fail the stronger monotone
requirement; observed order statistics closed under rank reversal provide
a construction that meets it. We also derive exact AIPW coding contrasts
for estimates and standard errors, and characterize the symmetric penalty
as an infimal convolution of anchored penalties. The prediction pilot finds
no general risk advantage over the alternatives. A known-effect causal
pilot reproduces coding agreement for the invariant alternatives, with
accuracy rankings that depend on the causal estimator. Coding invariance
is a separate property from accuracy and interval coverage.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An estimate of a treatment effect should not depend on whether a covariate
is recorded with one sign or its opposite. Such a recoding changes neither
the available information nor the causal contrast. Yet it can alter the
regularization of fitted outcome regressions and propensity scores, and
those changes can persist after targeted estimation. In the application
below, the sign and nominal interval of an ATT estimate change when four
continuous covariates are negated, despite common observations, folds, and
estimation settings.

The Highly Adaptive Lasso (HAL; \citealp{benkeser2016,vanderlaan2017})
provides a concrete setting for studying this dependence. In an anchored
zero-order dictionary, every indicator points in the same direction.
Reversing a covariate changes which functions have inexpensive coefficient
representations. Hardy--Krause variation's dependence on its anchor is
established \citep{aistleitner2015,aistleitner2017}. The causal application
uses a first-order implementation, so its sensitivity measures the whole
pipeline: basis construction, penalties, tuning, and optimization. An exact
univariate example isolates a first-order penalty mechanism: reflection
changes which endpoint slope is penalized. The multivariate analysis develops
a zero-order remedy. Neither calculation decomposes every feature of the
software comparison.

Coding dependence under regularization is not specific to HAL.
\citet{detmer2020} show that categorical group-lasso fits can depend on
the coding scheme and, under reference coding, on the reference category.
This illustrates the distinction between preserving the information or
linear predictor space and preserving a penalized fit. Here we examine
how coding dependence reaches causal estimates and their uncertainty,
and give conditions on the complete fitting procedure that prevent it.
These conditions concern functional predictions, including at held-out
points, rather than equality of training fits alone.

The primary requirement is that equivalent expressions of the covariates
give the same analysis. Making this requirement precise comes before
comparing estimators: we must specify the admissible recodings and the
outputs required to agree, including predictions at unobserved covariates
and reported uncertainty. Lower prediction error is a separate possible
property, not a condition for valuing coding invariance. A fixed sample can
demonstrate failure of invariance; known-truth experiments are needed to
assess its accuracy tradeoffs.

A symmetric dictionary is one way to obtain invariance. We retain distinct
functional atoms even when their training columns agree and select the
coefficient vector of smallest Euclidean norm among primary lasso minimizers.
Minimum-norm selection and its vanishing-ridge characterization are
established results \citep[Lemmas 9--10]{tibshirani2013}; we use them to make
predictions equivariant under reflection. A simpler alternative first chooses
a common orientation from the training covariates and averages directions
when the orientation rule is ambiguous. Thus symmetry of the dictionary is
a sufficient construction, not a necessary condition for invariant predictions.

The symmetric penalty has a separate property: different components can use
different anchors. An exact finite-grid example has lower symmetric penalty
than its penalty at every single anchor. We characterize this distinction
and give a lattice risk bound, while keeping those mathematical statements
separate from comparative risk claims for the cross-validated implementation.
An exploratory prediction study compares canonical orientation, anchor
selection and averaging, and symmetric HAL on common simulation draws.
It finds no general risk advantage for the symmetric fit. A paired
known-effect causal pilot checks coding movement and reports bias, RMSE,
and coverage with Monte Carlo uncertainty. Its accuracy comparisons remain
exploratory and do not validate interval coverage.

\section{Equivalent codings and the invariance requirement}\label{sec:recodings}

Let $D=\{(W_i,A_i,Y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, keeping treatment and outcome definitions
fixed. A covariate recoding $T$ preserves information if it is a measurable
bijection between the original support and the coded support, with a
measurable inverse. Then $\sigma\{T(W)\}=\sigma(W)$: each representation
determines the other. This includes a one-to-one categorical encoding on
its valid image, even when the ambient dimension changes. Binning, rounding,
and squaring a variable with both positive and negative support generally
fail this condition. They are outside the present requirement.

Information preservation leaves the causal functional unchanged. If
$W'=T(W)$, then
\[
 Q_a^T(w')=Q_a\{T^{-1}(w')\},\qquad
 g^T(w')=g\{T^{-1}(w')\}.
\]
Consequently $E\{Q_1^T(W')-Q_0^T(W')\mid A=1\}=\psi$.
Conditional exchangeability and overlap are likewise unchanged: the
conditioning information is the same. This does not establish those
assumptions in a given application.

\subsection{Which transformations should an estimator respect?}

Information equivalence alone does not specify the measurement structure
that a learner should use. Two useful requirements for numerical covariates
are nested. Coordinatewise affine recodings,
\[
 \mathcal G_{\rm aff}=\{T:T_j(w_j)=a_j+b_jw_j,\ b_j\ne0\},
\]
cover changes of units, zero points, and polarity, including a binary-label
swap. Coordinatewise strictly monotone recodings form a larger family,
$\mathcal G_{\rm ord}$, preserving coordinate order up to reversal. This
includes logarithms on positive support and cubic transformations on the
real line. Such transformations preserve information while changing
distances and spacings. Requiring invariance to them makes those spacings
irrelevant to the estimator. Nominal-category permutations and invertible
transformations that mix coordinates are additional requirements; neither
is automatically covered by a coordinatewise monotone guarantee.
Section~\ref{sec:nominal} gives an exact nominal-relabeling counterexample.

There is a concrete obstruction to requiring invariance under every
information-preserving map. Consider a deterministic predictor that receives
the coded sample and query, without an inverse map to a privileged coding.
A bijection can swap two covariate values absent from the training sample
while fixing every observed value. The training data then remain unchanged.
Pointwise equivariance under that bijection forces equal predictions at the
two unseen values. Under all coordinatewise bijections this argument applies
to each coordinate with the others held fixed. Thus universal pointwise
invariance discards distinctions among unseen levels, including order and
distance. The admissible family must be stated; information preservation
is necessary to our question, but does not by itself select that family.

\subsection{Predictions, estimates, and uncertainty}

Let $U$ record row-attached folds and any auxiliary randomization. For a
declared family $\mathcal G$, require
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prediction-equivariance}
 \widehat Q_a(TD,U)(Tw)=\widehat Q_a(D,U)(w),\qquad
 \widehat g(TD,U)(Tw)=\widehat g(D,U)(w),
 \quad T\in\mathcal G.
\end{equation}
This is a finite-sample requirement on the entire fitted function, not only
its values at training observations. For scalar outputs, require the point
estimate, standard error, and interval endpoints to remain unchanged.
Coupling $U$ separates coding dependence from changes in random splits.

\begin{proposition}[Transfer to downstream inference]\label{prop:transfer}
Suppose the nuisance fits satisfy \eqref{eq:prediction-equivariance} on
every outer training sample. Suppose subsequent clipping, calibration,
targeting, and inference are deterministic functions of the nuisance
predictions, $(A_i,Y_i)$, and common settings. Then their point estimates,
influence-function standard errors, and confidence intervals are unchanged
under every $T\in\mathcal G$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each held-out nuisance prediction is unchanged. Every subsequent step
therefore receives identical inputs and returns identical outputs.
\end{proof}
The same argument applies to a bootstrap with common resampling indices if
the full procedure is equivariant on every resample. Invariance of an
interval is distinct from correct coverage. Targeting need not repair a
failure of nuisance equivariance, and cancellation can make a scalar
estimate invariant even when its nuisances are not.

For a fixed sample, define the coding range
\[
 \Delta_{\mathcal G}(D,U)=
 \sup_{T,S\in\mathcal G}|\widehat\psi(TD,U)-\widehat\psi(SD,U)|.
\]
The requirement is $\Delta_{\mathcal G}=0$. A finite collection of recodings
gives a lower bound on this range and can refute invariance without knowing
the true treatment effect. Simulations can subsequently study the frequency
and magnitude of departures under specified populations, or the accuracy
and computation of invariant alternatives. They are not needed to define
the property or to establish its value as independence from coding choices.

\subsection{How nuisance changes reach AIPW inference}

An exact finite-sample calculation identifies conditions that can amplify
coding dependence. Keep $(A_i,Y_i)$ fixed, let $N_1=\sum_i A_i$,
$N_0=n-N_1$, and assume both groups are present. Write $q_i,g_i$ and
$q_i',g_i'$ for the effective untreated-mean and propensity predictions
under two codings, with $0\le g_i,g_i'<1$. These are the predictions actually
passed to AIPW, after any preparation and with outcomes in their original
units. Define $\delta q_i=q_i'-q_i$ and $\delta g_i=g_i'-g_i$.

\begin{proposition}[Propagation through AIPW]\label{prop:aipw-change}
For the ATT estimator
\[
 \widehat\psi(q,g)=\frac{1}{N_1}\sum_i
 \left\{A_i-(1-A_i)\frac{g_i}{1-g_i}\right\}(Y_i-q_i),
\]
let $\Delta\widehat\psi=\widehat\psi(q',g')-\widehat\psi(q,g)$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aipw-change}
 z_i=\left\{-A_i+(1-A_i)\frac{g_i'}{1-g_i'}\right\}\delta q_i
 -(1-A_i)(Y_i-q_i)
       \frac{\delta g_i}{(1-g_i)(1-g_i')}.
\end{equation}
Then $\Delta\widehat\psi=N_1^{-1}\sum_i z_i$. For the empirical influence
values
\[
 IC_i=\frac{A_i(Y_i-q_i-\widehat\psi)
 -(1-A_i)g_i(Y_i-q_i)/(1-g_i)}{p},\qquad p=N_1/n,
\]
and $\widehat{SE}=\operatorname{sd}(IC)/\sqrt n$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aipw-se-change}
 \Delta IC_i=\frac{z_i-A_i\Delta\widehat\psi}{p},\qquad
 |\widehat{SE}'-\widehat{SE}|
 \le\frac{\operatorname{sd}(\Delta IC)}{\sqrt n}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Subtract the two estimator numerators and use
$g_i'/(1-g_i')-g_i/(1-g_i)
=\delta g_i/\{(1-g_i)(1-g_i')\}$.
Subtracting the influence values gives the second identity. Sample standard
deviation is a centered Euclidean norm divided by $\sqrt{n-1}$, so its reverse
triangle inequality gives the bound.
\end{proof}

The decomposition changes the propensity first, then the outcome regression;
reversing this order reallocates their interaction. Its signed components
are accounting identities, not unique causal attributions. The triangle
inequality gives
$|\Delta\widehat\psi|\le N_1^{-1}\sum_i|z_i|\le B_q+B_g$, where
\begin{align*}
 B_q&=\frac{1}{N_1}\sum_i
 \left\{A_i+(1-A_i)\frac{g_i'}{1-g_i'}\right\}|\delta q_i|,\\
 B_g&=\frac{1}{N_1}\sum_i
 \frac{(1-A_i)|Y_i-q_i|\,|\delta g_i|}{(1-g_i)(1-g_i')}.
\end{align*}
If both propensities are at most $1-\eta$, the prediction changes are bounded
by $\epsilon_q,\epsilon_g$, and control residuals satisfy $|Y_i-q_i|\le M$,
then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aipw-overlap-bound}
 |\Delta\widehat\psi|\le
 \epsilon_q\left\{1+\frac{N_0}{N_1}\frac{1-\eta}{\eta}\right\}
 +\frac{N_0}{N_1}\frac{M\epsilon_g}{\eta^2}.
\end{equation}
Thus small nuisance changes alone do not ensure small ATT movement. Weak
control overlap, large control residuals, and a small treated fraction can
amplify them. The bound is sufficient, not a prediction of movement: signed
contributions can cancel. Propensity calibration can exceed an initial
clipping ceiling, so $\eta$ must describe the effective predictions.

Cancellation can also hide changes in uncertainty. For
$A=(1,1,0,0)$, $Y=(1,-1,1,-1)$, and $g=g'=(1/2,1/2,1/2,1/2)$,
take $q=(0,0,0,0)$ and $q'=(1,1,1,1)$. Both ATT estimates are zero, but
their influence values are $(2,-2,-2,2)$ and $(0,-4,0,4)$, respectively.
The standard errors are $\sqrt{4/3}$ and $\sqrt{8/3}$. Checking only
point-estimate agreement therefore misses a failure of inference invariance.
These results concern AIPW; they supply neither a quantitative TMLE bound
nor a coverage guarantee.

\subsection{Sources of coding dependence}

The requirement identifies separate places where the pipeline can change.
Basis functions or knots may fail to transform into the same functions.
Even when their span is unchanged, the coefficient penalty may assign
different costs to the same function. Nonunique primary solutions can lead
to different predictions away from the training sample. Tuning may change
through folds, candidate penalties, validation predictions, or tie rules.
Finally, a theoretically equivariant procedure can lose numerical
equivariance through tolerances, path truncation, or floating-point
comparisons. These mechanisms call for distinct checks. Representation-cost
calculations concern the penalty; held-out knot counterexamples concern
the function space; solver-path controls concern numerical implementation.

\subsection{A first-order penalty mechanism}\label{sec:firstorder-penalty}

Coding dependence can arise even in one dimension with first-order splines.
Fix $a<t_1<\cdots<t_m<b$ and write $u_+=\max(u,0)$. Consider continuous
piecewise-linear functions on $[a,b]$ with knots among the $t_j$. The
left-anchored basis is $x-a,(x-t_1)_+,\ldots,(x-t_m)_+$, and the
right-anchored basis is $b-x,(t_1-x)_+,\ldots,(t_m-x)_+$. Both include a
free constant and penalize every other coefficient with unit weight.
Reflection interchanges these bases after mapping the knots and domain.
They span exactly the same functions.

\begin{proposition}[Endpoint slopes in the first-order penalty]
\label{prop:firstorder-penalty}
For such a function $f$, let
$\gamma_j=f'(t_j+)-f'(t_j-)$ be its slope jumps. The two representation
penalties are
\[
 V_L(f)=|f'(a+)|+\sum_j|\gamma_j|,\qquad
 V_R(f)=|f'(b-)|+\sum_j|\gamma_j|.
\]
In particular, $f(x)=(x-t)_+$ for $a<t<b$ has $V_L(f)=1$ and $V_R(f)=2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The left linear coefficient is $f'(a+)$, and each hinge coefficient is
the corresponding slope jump. On the right, the linear coefficient is
$-f'(b-)$ and the hinge coefficients are the same slope jumps. The constant,
endpoint slope, and jumps uniquely determine the function, proving both
formulas. A single upper hinge has left slope zero, right slope one, and
one unit jump.
\end{proof}

The different penalties can produce different exact fits on the same sample.
Take $x=(0,1/2,1)$, $y=(0,0,1/2)$, one interior knot at $1/2$, and
$\lambda=1/216$ in the squared-error objective~\eqref{eq:lasso}. In the
left basis $(x,(x-1/2)_+)$, the unique solution has intercept zero and
coefficients $(1/18,5/6)$, giving fitted values
$(0,1/36,17/36)$. In the right basis $(1-x,(1/2-x)_+)$, the unique
solution has intercept $4/9$ and coefficients $(-13/18,1/2)$, giving
$(-1/36,1/12,4/9)$. In each case the residual mean is zero and
$X^\top(\hat f-y)/3=-\lambda\operatorname{sign}(\hat\beta)$, which
certifies optimality. The design including the intercept has full rank,
so each solution is unique. Neither nonunique solutions nor optimization
error is needed for coding dependence in this example.

The first-order basis generated by \texttt{hal9001} version \HalVersion{}
on these three covariate values gives these same atoms, plus an endpoint
atom identically zero on the domain. Its no-formula fit uses unit penalty
factors and disables feature standardization \citep{hal9001fit}. The example
fixes the knots and penalty; it is not a reproduction of the package's
default cross-validation. It establishes a possible first-order penalty
mechanism, not its contribution to the observational result below. If
affine functions are unpenalized, both penalties instead reduce to
$\sum_j|\gamma_j|$. That removes this univariate endpoint-slope mechanism.

The latter penalty is established in the spline literature. The univariate
MARS-LASSO formulation of \citet[Section~4.1]{ki2024} penalizes the total
variation of the derivative and leaves the linear component unpenalized.
Its endpoint convention therefore differs from the unit-penalty example
above. Their construction also scales covariates to a common interval
before penalization. Reflection invariance of derivative variation alone
does not give unit invariance: for $T(x)=a+bx$, $b\ne0$, the derivative
variation of $f\circ T^{-1}$ is $\operatorname{TV}(f')/|b|$ on the
transformed domain. Scale handling, knots, and tuning remain part of the
fitting procedure. Neither the univariate calculation nor its
unpenalized-linear alternative establishes invariance of a complete
multivariate implementation.

\section{Coding sensitivity in treatment-effect estimation}\label{sec:causal}

Negating continuous covariates changes the ATT TMLE from
$\TmleOriginal$ to $\TmleReflected$ dollars, a movement of
$\TmleMovement$ dollars (Table~\ref{tab:causal}). The outcome, treatment,
sample membership, folds, nuisance learner, clipping rules, and targeting
settings are held fixed. Both fits pass the numerical targeting checks
below and their nuisance fits produce no convergence warnings. This is a
sensitivity result for the specified estimation pipeline; the observational
comparison does not establish which estimate is causally accurate.

The sample combines the Dehejia--Wahba treated group with the PSID comparison
group \citep{lalonde1986,dehejia1999}: \CausalTreated{} treated observations
among \CausalN{} observations. The outcome is 1978 earnings. Covariates are
age, education, indicators for being Black, Hispanic, married, or without
a degree, and earnings in 1974 and 1975. The reflected coding negates age,
education, and both pretreatment earnings variables. A unit-change coding
expresses age in months and pretreatment earnings in thousands of dollars.
All three transformations preserve information and the treatment contrast.

Write $A$ for training participation, $Y$ for earnings, and $W$ for the
covariates. With $Q_a(w)=E[Y\mid A=a,W=w]$, the statistical target is
$\psi=E[Q_1(W)-Q_0(W)\mid A=1]$. Under consistency, conditional
exchangeability of the untreated potential outcome, and control overlap
on the treated support, this equals $E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid A=1]$.
The coding comparison holds that functional and sample fixed; it does not
test these identifying assumptions.

For each coding, five outer folds produce held-out predictions of
$Q_0$, $Q_1$, and $g(w)=P(A=1\mid W=w)$.
Each training fit uses ten common inner folds. Nuisance estimation uses
\texttt{hal9001} version \HalVersion{} with its default first-order basis,
maximum interaction degree three, knot counts, and penalty selection
\citep{hejazi2020}. These are package-default fits, distinct from the
zero-order symmetric implementation developed below. The comparison measures
the behavior of the whole fitted procedure and does not isolate the
penalty from knot construction, tuning, or numerical optimization.

Raw AIPW uses the held-out $Q_0$ predictions and caps $g$ at $0.99$:
\[
 \hat\psi_{\mathrm{AIPW}}=
 \frac{\sum_i\{A_i(Y_i-\hat Q_{0i})-
 (1-A_i)\hat g_i(1-\hat g_i)^{-1}(Y_i-\hat Q_{0i})\}}
 {\sum_i A_i}.
\]
For the common-initial-nuisance comparison, rescale outcomes to $[0,1]$
using their observed range \citep{gruber2010} and clip each outcome prediction to
$[10^{-6},1-10^{-6}]$. Initially clip propensities to $[0.01,0.99]$, then
fit a pooled logistic intercept with the clipped log odds as offset, so
the mean initial propensity equals the observed treatment proportion.
The calibrated AIPW and TMLE start from these same prepared predictions.
Reported estimates and standard errors are transformed back to dollars.

The ATT TMLE uses the exported \texttt{oneStepATT} implementation in
\texttt{tmle} version \TmleVersion{}, which jointly targets the outcome regressions
and propensity score through a universal least-favorable submodel
\citep{vanderlaan2016}. We supply the entire sample without the trimming
performed by the package's high-level ATT wrapper. Initial nuisance fits
are cross-fitted; calibration and targeting use the pooled sample.
A numerical propensity floor of $10^{-8}$ and outcome-probability bounds
$[10^{-12},1-10^{-12}]$ during targeting are separate from the initial
clipping rules. The smaller outcome bound lets initially clipped predictions
move along the targeting path. Reusing the initial bound can block that
motion, so a forward loss-stop flag alone does not establish a solved
influence-function equation. We compare step sizes $10^{-4}$,
$10^{-5}$, and $10^{-6}$ and report the smallest-step fit. Acceptance
requires convergence, an absolute empirical influence-function mean below
$0.01$ standard errors, a final refinement change below $0.01$ standard
errors, and a relative standard-error change below $0.01$.

\subsection{Observed AIPW changes and cancellation}

Applying Proposition~\ref{prop:aipw-change} to the saved predictions gives
the exact decomposition in Table~\ref{tab:causal-contrast}. Sign reversal
increases raw AIPW by $\AipwSignMovement$ dollars while decreasing its
standard error by $\AipwSignSEMovement$ dollars. The bound $B_q+B_g$ is
$\AipwSignBound$ dollars, much larger than the net movement. Summing the
complete row changes $z_i/N_1$ separately by sign gives
$\AipwSignPositive$ and $\AipwSignNegative$ dollars. These totals do not
depend on the substitution order and establish cancellation across rows.
A small net change therefore need not imply small changes in individual
contributions.

The largest propensity change is $\AipwSignMaxG$, and the largest untreated
outcome-prediction change is $\AipwSignMaxQ$ dollars. Thus the observed
comparison does not represent only tiny prediction perturbations amplified
by poor overlap. The algebra explains how the effective nuisance changes
reach AIPW and its standard error, without identifying which part of nuisance
fitting produced those changes. The attribution between the $q$ and $g$
terms depends on the substitution order; the reproducible output also
reports the reverse order. These AIPW calculations do not explain the larger
TMLE sign reversal, whose targeting map is different.

\subsection{Solver and scaling controls}

We repeat the first-order analysis with a tighter convergence threshold
and larger iteration budget, with covariate standardization, and with both
controls (Table~\ref{tab:causal-controls}). Standardization subtracts each
outer training sample's column means and divides by its column standard
deviations; the same transformation is applied to held-out observations.
The tighter solver uses threshold $10^{-12}$ and iteration limit $10^6$.
Observations, folds, and targeting settings remain common across codings.

Standardization removes the observed unit dependence to numerical precision
at both solver settings. Across these two controls, the largest absolute
unit-change movement is $\StandardizedUnitATTMovement$ dollars in the
TMLE and $\StandardizedUnitSEMovement$ dollars in its standard error.
Tighter optimization alone leaves substantial unit dependence.

The point-estimate sign reversal persists under each control. Nuisance
convergence warnings also persist, including under the combined controls.
Thus the reversal is not removed by these changes in settings, but the
comparison does not establish that incomplete optimization is irrelevant.
The large, coding-dependent standard errors under standardization further
separate reproducibility of a procedure from precision of its estimate.

\subsection{A numerical mechanism in a tighter-solver control}

Increasing solver accuracy can expose a separate difficulty in penalty
selection. With automatic penalties, \texttt{cv.glmnet} fits a master path
and separate paths in its validation folds, then aligns predictions to the
master penalties \citep{glmnetcv}. A fold whose path ends before a requested
penalty can return its last available fit. Successful optimization of the
selected master fit therefore does not establish that every validation
fold reached the same penalty.

We reproduce this mechanism in a tighter-solver version of the original
coding, with convergence threshold $10^{-12}$ and iteration limit $10^6$.
For $Q_1$ in outer fold one, inner fold four stops at
$\PathLastLambda$, above the selected master penalty
$\PathSelectedLambda$. Its predictions at the selected penalty equal its
endpoint predictions exactly. Extending that fold's path with an iteration
limit of $10^7$, while holding its basis and convergence threshold fixed,
changes validation predictions by as much as $\PathPredictionMovement$
dollars. Predictions between fitted penalties use the package's linear
interpolation rule \citep{glmnetpredict}. In contrast, refitting the master
path through its selected penalty reproduces its saved held-out predictions
within $\PathMasterMovement$ dollars; its maximum KKT residual, divided by
the corresponding centered feature RMS and the outcome standard deviation,
is $\PathMasterKKT$.

This is a verified validation-path artifact in one component of the
tighter control. It does not measure its frequency, demonstrate a change
in the selected penalty, or explain the sign reversal under default
settings. The distinction matters when interpreting tighter fits as a
control for optimization: completing a master fit and completing the
validation paths are different requirements.

\subsection{Canonical orientation in the application}

Standardization followed by canonical orientation makes the first-order
ATT TMLE reproducible across the three codings. The estimate is
$\CanonicalATT$ dollars under each coding (Table~\ref{tab:causal-canonical}).
The sign-reversal movement is $\CanonicalSignMovement$ and the unit-change
movement is $\CanonicalUnitMovement$ dollars. The wrapper uses training
covariates to choose an orientation before invoking the same first-order

\section{A known-effect reference distribution}\label{sec:known-effect}

To assess accuracy separately from coding reproducibility, we construct a
population with a known ATT. Its covariate distribution is the empirical
distribution of the \CausalN{} application rows. A logistic regression of
treatment on the covariates, age squared, and zero-earnings indicators defines
$g(W)$, capped at $0.95$. A control-group regression with natural splines in
age and both pretreatment earnings variables, plus the remaining covariates
and zero-earnings indicators, defines $Q_0(W)$. These fitted functions and the
control-regression residual standard deviation $\sigma$ are held fixed.
Each simulated sample draws covariate rows with replacement, draws
$A\mid W\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}\{g(W)\}$, and sets
\[
 Y=Q_0(W)+1800A+\epsilon,\qquad
 \epsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^2),
\]
independently across observations. The ATT is exactly $1800$. The Gaussian
model permits negative outcomes; it calibrates an earnings-like outcome
without reproducing the full empirical earnings distribution. Outcome
truncation would change this data-generating model and is not applied.

The oracle calculation uses the true $Q_0$ and $g$ in raw AIPW. For
$N_1=\sum_i A_i>0$, its error satisfies the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oracle-noise}
 \hat\psi_{\mathrm{oracle}}-1800
 =\frac{1}{N_1}\sum_i
 \left\{A_i-(1-A_i)\frac{g(W_i)}{1-g(W_i)}\right\}\epsilon_i.
\end{equation}
Conditional on the sampled covariates and treatments, this error has mean
zero and is Gaussian, with variance
\[
 \operatorname{Var}(\hat\psi_{\mathrm{oracle}}\mid W,A)
 =\frac{\sigma^2}{N_1^2}
   \sum_i\left\{A_i+(1-A_i)
       \left(\frac{g(W_i)}{1-g(W_i)}\right)^2\right\}.
\]
This provides an exact conditional interval benchmark. The nonparametric
ATT influence-function variance at this population is
$\sigma^2 E\{g(W)/(1-g(W))\}/p^2$, where $p=E\{g(W)\}$. Expectations are
computed over the fixed empirical covariate population. The corresponding
asymptotic standard-error benchmark at the application sample size is
$\OracleEfficiencySE$ outcome units; it is distinct from the conditional
variance for a particular simulated sample.

Across \OracleReplicates{} independent oracle draws, estimated bias is
$\OracleBias$ with Monte Carlo standard error $\OracleBiasMCSE$, and the
sampling standard deviation is $\OracleSD$. Empirical influence-function
intervals cover the true ATT in $\OracleCoverage\%$ of draws, compared with
$\OracleConditionalCoverage\%$ for intervals using the known conditional
variance (Table~\ref{tab:oracle}). This benchmark checks the generator and
oracle inference calculation. It does not validate intervals after nuisance
estimation, propensity calibration, or TMLE targeting. In particular,
oracle-initialized TMLE modifies its starting nuisance functions and is a
different estimator from the raw oracle AIPW benchmark.

\section{Zero-order dictionaries and estimators}

Let $x \in \R^d$. For a \emph{section} $s \subseteq [d]$, a knot
$u \in \R^{s}$ and an \emph{orientation} $o \in \{+,-\}^{s}$, define the
feature
\[
  \phi_{s,u,o}(x) = \prod_{j \in s} h_{o_j}(x_j - u_j), \qquad
  h_+(t) = \one\{t \ge 0\}, \quad h_-(t) = \one\{t \le 0\}.
\]
The \emph{anchored dictionary} $\mathcal{A}$ contains the features with
$o \equiv +$; this is the zero-order basis of the Highly Adaptive Lasso
(HAL; \citealp{vanderlaan2017,benkeser2016}). The \emph{symmetric dictionary}
$\mathcal{S}$ contains all orientations. Both are restricted to
$1 \le |s| \le D$ and to knots on a grid $K = \prod_j K_j$.
Reflection $x_j \mapsto -x_j$ maps $\mathcal{S}$ onto itself (with
$K_j \mapsto -K_j$); it does not map $\mathcal{A}$ onto itself.

For a dictionary $\mathcal{D}$, the \emph{penalty} of a function $f$ is the
smallest $\ell_1$ norm over representations, with the constant unpenalized:
\[
  V_{\mathcal{D}}(f) = \inf\Big\{ \textstyle\sum_{\phi} |\beta_\phi| :
  f = \beta_0 + \sum_{\phi \in \mathcal{D}} \beta_\phi \phi \Big\},
\]
with $V_{\mathcal{D}}(f) = \infty$ if no representation exists. Since
$\mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathcal{S}$, $V_{\mathcal{S}} \le V_{\mathcal{A}}$.
On a full finite product grid, remove anchored atoms whose threshold in
an active coordinate equals that coordinate's minimum: they duplicate a
lower-dimensional atom or the constant. The resulting tensor dictionary
is linearly independent together with the constant, so its representation
is unique. With full interaction degree $D=d$, this finite-grid penalty is
the discrete counterpart of Hardy--Krause variation anchored at the lower
corner. For right-continuous functions of bounded Hardy--Krause variation
on $[0,1]^d$, the continuum representation uses integrals against finite
signed measures, with their total variation
replacing coefficient sums and the constant unpenalized
\citep{aistleitner2015,fang2021}. Simply allowing more knots in a finite
coefficient sum does not define that continuum extension. Agreement of columns
on a training sample alone does not imply equality of the represented
functions away from that sample.

Given data $(x_i, y_i)_{i=1}^n$ and a finite dictionary with design
$X \in \R^{n \times p}$, the lasso estimator is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lasso}
  (\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta) \in \argmin_{\beta_0,\beta}
  \tfrac{1}{2n}\|y - \beta_0\one - X\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|_1 .
\end{equation}
HAL is \eqref{eq:lasso} on $\mathcal{A}$; \emph{symmetric HAL} is
\eqref{eq:lasso} on $\mathcal{S}$.

\section{Coding invariance}\label{sec:equiv}

Let $R$ negate any subset of coordinates. A knot rule is reflection
equivariant if applying it to $Rx$ gives the reflected knots. Type-7
quantiles at probability levels closed under $p\mapsto1-p$ satisfy this
condition. Require the retained \emph{functions}, not only their training
columns or spans, to correspond bijectively under $\phi\mapsto\phi\circ R$.
Filtering on the fraction of training observations where an atom is one
preserves this correspondence. Keeping the first of several identical
training columns need not preserve it.

\begin{proposition}[Equivariant solution selection]\label{prop:equiv}
Suppose the finite functional dictionaries before and after a recoding $T$
correspond by a permutation $\pi$ satisfying
$\phi^T_{\pi(j)}(Tx)=\phi_j(x)$ at every point, with the same coefficient
penalties and a free intercept.
For $\lambda>0$, first minimize \eqref{eq:lasso}, then minimize
$\|\beta\|_2^2$ over its minimizers. The resulting coefficient vector and
intercept are unique, and the fitted functions satisfy
$\hat f^{T}(Tx)=\hat f(x)$ at every point, including points outside the
training sample. The same statement holds for binomial negative
log-likelihood when both response classes occur. Cross-validation preserves
the result if folds, candidate penalties, and the rule for resolving equal
validation losses are the same under the recoding, with an equivariant
dictionary construction inside each training fold. For binomial fits,
both response classes must occur in every training fold and in the final
training sample.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The recoding permutes the functional atoms and their coefficients. Both the
primary objective and the secondary squared coefficient norm are unchanged
by this permutation. Positive $\lambda$ bounds the penalized coefficients
on a primary sublevel set. Gaussian loss, or binomial loss with both classes
present, then bounds the intercept, so a primary minimizer exists. Strict
convexity of the loss in the linear predictors makes the vector of fitted
linear predictors unique. All primary minimizers therefore also have the
same $\ell_1$ norm. Their coefficient vectors form a convex compact set,
on which the strictly convex secondary norm has a unique minimizer; its
intercept is determined by the fitted linear predictors. The permutation
must map these unique solutions to one another. Held-out predictions and
validation losses consequently agree, so a common deterministic tuning rule
and refit preserve the conclusion.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Affine recodings]\label{cor:affine}
Under the solution-selection and tuning conditions of
Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv}, the ideal zero-order symmetric estimator
underlying the implementation is equivariant under $\mathcal G_{\rm aff}$
with exact arithmetic and exact primary and secondary minimization. This holds
for the symmetric type-7 quantile probability grid, or for supplied knots
transformed along with their covariates.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For $b>0$, a type-7 sample quantile satisfies
$q_p(a+bX)=a+bq_p(X)$. For $b<0$ it satisfies
$q_p(a+bX)=a+bq_{1-p}(X)$. The chosen probability grid is closed under
$p\mapsto1-p$, so its knot set transforms accordingly. Positive scales
preserve an indicator's orientation; negative scales reverse it. The full
functional dictionary is therefore permuted. The primary objective,
secondary coefficient norm, and held-out validation losses are preserved,
so the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv} applies.
\end{proof}

This result requires a solution-selection rule. The usual lasso guarantees
unique training predictions, but may have several coefficient vectors
\citep{tibshirani2013}; their predictions elsewhere can differ. For example,
when training observations satisfy $x_1=x_2$, indicators on the two
coordinates can agree on every training row but differ off that diagonal.
Reflection can change which representative survives deduplication or which
solution a coordinate-descent solver selects. Retaining all functional
atoms resolves the first problem, but does not by itself resolve the second.

The implementation retains the full degree-at-most-two dictionary. It solves
the primary problem with \texttt{glmnet}, then minimizes coefficient norm
on the equicorrelation/sign face with the training linear predictors fixed,
using \texttt{quadprog}. Face construction accounts for primary numerical
error. Full-dictionary optimality residuals, prediction residuals, and
preservation of the primary $\ell_1$ norm and objective are checked; a
solution that fails these checks is rejected. Numerical residuals are not
universal bounds on held-out prediction error, which is checked separately
under reflection in the examples and tests.

\subsection{Canonical orientation as a simpler remedy}\label{sec:canonical}

Dictionary closure is sufficient for invariance, but is not necessary.
For a training sample $D$, let $m_j(D)$ depend only on coordinate $j$, be
unchanged by row permutations, and change sign when that coordinate is
reflected. An example is the
centered third moment, divided by the cube of the largest absolute centered
value; define it as zero for a constant column. Choose $s_j=\operatorname{sign}
m_j(D)$ when this score is nonzero, and fit the anchored learner to
$s\odot x$. Under a reflection $r$, the score and chosen direction both
change by $r_j$, leaving the oriented training and prediction inputs identical.
Any deterministic learner applied to these inputs therefore gives the same
predictions. This argument does not require a symmetric dictionary.

A fixed sign fallback at zero does not have that property. Instead, retain
both signs for every ambiguous coordinate and average the resulting
predictions. Under reflection the set of orientations is permuted, so the
average is unchanged. This requires one fit when all coordinates have a
nonzero score and up to $2^d$ fits when every coordinate is ambiguous.
This is a finite-sign instance of averaging over an equivariant,
data-dependent set of transformations, as in frame averaging
\citep{puny2021}.
For binomial outcomes, average probabilities. Our implementation treats
absolute normalized scores at most $10^{-12}$ as ambiguous, recomputes
orientations inside each validation training fold, and selects a common
penalty from the averaged held-out loss. In exact arithmetic the ambiguity
rule is reflection invariant; finite precision is assessed by prediction
checks. This preprocessing changes the anchored estimator's orientation,
whereas the symmetric dictionary changes its penalty by allowing components
to use different orientations.
The hard orientation rule can be discontinuous when its score crosses an
ambiguity threshold. Equivariance under recoding therefore does not imply
stability under small changes to the data, a distinction also emphasized
in the canonicalization literature \citep{dym2024}.

The same idea can wrap a first-order learner. Our first-order variant
centers and scales each covariate within an outer training sample, chooses
its canonical orientations, and fits the package-default learner separately
in each orientation, using common inner folds. It averages response
predictions, with separate penalty selection for each constituent fit.
Preprocessing uses the whole outer training sample; it is not recomputed
inside the package's inner folds. No outer held-out observations enter that
preprocessing. For coordinates with positive training variance, centering
and scaling remove affine recodings with nonzero scale up to sign in exact
arithmetic. Canonical orientation removes the remaining sign change,
conditional on deterministic fitting and common folds. This
wrapper differs from the zero-order comparator's fold-local orientations
and common-penalty ensemble tuning.

\subsection{The stronger requirement of monotone invariance}

Equivariance under $\mathcal G_{\rm ord}$ imposes an additional structural
restriction on prediction between observations.
\begin{proposition}[Prediction within observed gaps]\label{prop:ordinal-gaps}
Let a deterministic predictor on $\mathbb R^d$ be pointwise equivariant
under every coordinatewise strictly increasing bijection of $\mathbb R$.
Fix its training sample, auxiliary randomness, and all but one query
coordinate. Its prediction is constant on each open interval between
successive distinct observed values of that coordinate, and on each of
the two exterior intervals. More generally, it is constant on every
Cartesian product of these intervals and singleton observed values.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For two points in the same interval, there is a strictly increasing
piecewise-linear bijection that maps one to the other and fixes every
observed value. Apply this map in the selected coordinate and the identity
in the others. The training sample is unchanged, so equivariance equates
the predictions. Applying this argument one coordinate at a time proves
the product statement.
\end{proof}
The restriction allows jumps at observed values and imposes no equality
between a value and its neighboring open intervals. It is necessary, not
sufficient: a predictor with these constant pieces can still use spacings
to choose their heights. An interpolated knot can violate the necessary
condition even when it leaves the training design unchanged.

\begin{corollary}[Continuous predictions]\label{cor:continuous}
Under the assumptions of Proposition~\ref{prop:ordinal-gaps}, if the
fixed-sample predictor is continuous in each query coordinate, it is
constant as a function of the query on $\mathbb R^d$. The constant may
depend on the training sample and auxiliary randomness.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
On each coordinate line, continuity at every distinct observed value
equates the constants on its two neighboring intervals with the prediction
at that value. The predictor is therefore constant on the entire line.
Changing coordinates one at a time proves constancy on $\mathbb R^d$.
\end{proof}
A nonconstant continuous spline predictor cannot meet this full pointwise
monotone requirement. This does not preclude affine equivariance or smooth
estimation generally. A nonconstant invariant rank-based predictor must
instead be discontinuous somewhere in the original query coordinates;
rank preprocessing alone does not ensure invariance. The connected query
domain is essential: continuity on discrete or disconnected support need
not join the pieces. The result concerns a finite-sample prediction rule,
not information equivalence or identification of a population target.

For a concrete algebraic example, take one covariate with training values
$(0,2)$, outcomes $(0,1)$, three knots, and Gaussian penalty $\lambda=1/20$.
The original knots are $(1/2,1,3/2)$. After the increasing bijection
$T(x)=x^3$, type-7 knots are $(2,4,6)$, rather than the cubes of the original
knots. Both training design matrices are identical. Their common
minimum-norm lasso solution has intercept $1/2$ and coefficient $2/15$ on
each upper indicator and $-2/15$ on each lower indicator. Its training
predictions are $(1/10,9/10)$; the Gaussian KKT equations hold with
equality. Any coefficient vector giving that fitted contrast has squared
norm at least $(4/5)^2/6$ by Cauchy--Schwarz, and these coefficients attain it.
Nevertheless, the original prediction at $3/4$ is $11/30$, while the
recoded prediction at $T(3/4)$ is $1/10$. This is a failure of the stronger
invariance requirement without any loss of information or optimization error.

An order-statistic knot rule supplies a sufficient construction.
\begin{corollary}[Monotone recodings]\label{cor:ordinal}
For each training size $n$, fix a nonempty rank set
$R_n\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}$, independently of the observed values, with
$R_n=\{n+1-r:r\in R_n\}$. In coordinate $j$, use the distinct values in
$\{W_{(r)j}:r\in R_n\}$ as knots, and include both closed indicator
orientations for every retained section. Under the solution-selection
and tuning conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv}, this ideal
zero-order symmetric estimator is equivariant under $\mathcal G_{\rm ord}$.
The rank rule is reapplied at each training-fold size. The conclusion
allows tied observations and constant coordinates.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For increasing $T_j$, the $r$th order statistic becomes $T_j(W_{(r)j})$;
for decreasing $T_j$, it becomes $T_j(W_{(n+1-r)j})$. Rank reversal closure
therefore makes the knot sets correspond, including when several selected
ranks give the same value. Injectivity preserves equality, so removing
repeated knot values commutes with the recoding. Increasing maps preserve
the two indicator inequalities and decreasing maps exchange them. Products
over unchanged coordinate sections thus give a permutation of the full
functional dictionary. Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv} applies in every
training fold and in the final fit.
\end{proof}
For example, when selecting central ranks in an even sample, retain both
observed values rather than averaging them. Removing duplicate knot values
is permissible; discarding distinct functions merely because their training
columns agree is not covered. The implementation's
\texttt{knot\_rule = "observed"} option uses
\[
 r_k=1+\left\lfloor\frac{(n-1)k}{q+1}\right\rfloor,\qquad
 R_n=\{r_k,n+1-r_k:k=1,\ldots,q\}.
\]
For a requested count $q$, this gives at most $2q$ distinct knots per
coordinate. The rule is rebuilt inside each training fold and in the final
fit. Its ideal estimator satisfies the corollary; the interpolated-quantile
default does not. With equivariant nuisance fitting,
Proposition~\ref{prop:transfer} also gives invariance of downstream effect
estimates and uncertainty under this larger family.

The canonical comparator also changes under the stronger requirement.
Its third-moment orientation statistic uses spacings, which an increasing
nonlinear transformation can alter. An unsupervised marginal rule cannot
simply discard spacings while retaining its usual single orientation.
\begin{proposition}[Marginal ordinal orientations]\label{prop:ordinal-frame}
Let a nonempty orientation set $S(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\subseteq\{-1,+1\}$
be unchanged by row permutations and strictly increasing recodings, and
satisfy $S(-x)=-S(x)$. If the observed values are distinct, then
$S(x)=\{-1,+1\}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For sorted values $x_{(1)}<\cdots<x_{(n)}$, construct a strictly increasing
bijection $h$ with $h(x_{(i)})=-x_{(n+1-i)}$; piecewise-linear interpolation
and increasing tails provide one. Then $h(x)$ is a row permutation of $-x$.
The assumptions imply $S(x)=S(h(x))=S(-x)=-S(x)$. The only nonempty
subset of $\{-1,+1\}$ with that property contains both signs.
\end{proof}

A Cartesian product of such marginal orientation sets consequently has
$2^d$ elements when every coordinate has distinct observed values, as occurs
almost surely for an i.i.d. sample from continuous marginals. By comparison,
a symmetric dictionary
with at most $m$ knots per coordinate and maximum interaction degree $D$
has at most $1+\sum_{k=1}^D\binom{d}{k}(2m)^k$ columns including the
intercept, polynomial in $d$ and $m$ for fixed $D$. This compares dictionary
size with the cardinality of a marginal averaging construction. It is not
a lower bound on all canonicalization algorithms: joint or outcome-informed
rules, coincident fits, and shared computation fall outside that comparison.

\subsection{Nominal categories}\label{sec:nominal}

Arbitrary relabeling of nominal categories preserves information but can
change the ordering used by threshold functions. Symmetry under reversal
does not remove this dependence. Consider three levels $A,B,C$ coded
$0,1,2$, with one observation per level and both indicator orientations
at all three support values. This dictionary spans every function on the
finite support. The minimum coefficient penalty for values on this support,
with the intercept free, is
\[
 V(f)=|f_B-f_A|+|f_C-f_B|.
\]
Every nonconstant threshold atom has discrete total variation one, giving
the lower bound; the two successive increments of $f$ attain it.

With outcomes $(0,1,0)$ and $\lambda=1/18$ in the Gaussian
objective~\eqref{eq:lasso}, the unique fitted values in $A,B,C$ order are
$(1/6,2/3,1/6)$. Swap the numerical codes of $A$ and $B$, retaining the
outcomes and all support values as knots. The support order is now $B,A,C$,
and the fitted values in the original row order become
$(1/12,5/6,1/12)$. To verify optimality, let $D$ take adjacent differences
in support order. The KKT equation is
$(f-y)/3+\lambda D^\top s=0$, with
$s\in\partial\|Df\|_1$. The original solution satisfies it with
$s=(1,-1)$; the recoded solution, expressed in $B,A,C$ order, satisfies it
with $s=(-1,-1/2)$. Strict convexity in fitted values gives uniqueness.
Both designs are saturated and every category is observed. The change is
therefore a penalty effect, without information loss, off-sample ambiguity,
or numerical optimization error. Arbitrary nominal-label invariance requires
a construction that respects category permutations.

One sufficient construction represents each nominal variable by the full
vector of its category indicators, retaining a fixed category schema across
training folds and prediction inputs. Relabeling then permutes input columns.
If the same knot rule is applied to every column and all sections up to the
specified degree are included, this induces a permutation of functional atoms.
The coefficient penalties and minimum-norm selection are unchanged, so the
same equivariance argument applies, including cross-validation with common
folds and penalty candidates. This construction works for both anchored and
symmetric dictionaries; its label invariance does not depend on reflection
symmetry. It defines a different procedure from thresholding scalar category
codes. It gives no guarantee for changing the omitted reference category in
reduced indicator coding or for arbitrary contrast bases. The number of
indicator columns also increases the dictionary's computational cost.

\section{Penalty geometry}\label{sec:cost}

\subsection{What a box costs}

A box $B = \prod_{j \in s} I_j$ in section $s$ has, in each coordinate,
an interval $I_j$ that is \emph{upper} ($[a_j,\infty)$), \emph{lower}
($(-\infty,b_j]$) or \emph{two-sided} ($[a_j,b_j]$). Let $m,k,t$ count
the upper, lower and two-sided coordinates, $m+k+t=|s|$, with $|s| \le D$.
Take the domain to be a finite grid $G = \prod_j G_j$, such as a lattice
design or the observed values, with a knot at every grid point, so that
$\one\{x_j > b\} = h_+(x_j - b^+)$ where $b^+$ is the next point of $G_j$
above $b$; finite endpoints lie in their coordinate grids. First intersect
each interval with its grid. If any intersection is empty, $f=0$. Delete
coordinates whose interval covers the entire coordinate grid. Among the
remaining coordinates, call an interval upper or lower if it touches only
the top or bottom grid boundary, and two-sided if it touches neither.
Thus each counted face is an observable jump with grid points on both
sides. If no coordinates remain, the function is constant and both
penalties are zero. The following statement concerns the remaining
nonconstant, nonempty boxes.

\begin{proposition}[Penalty of a box]\label{prop:box}
For $f = c\,\one_B$ with $c \neq 0$,
\[
  V_{\mathcal{S}}(f) = |c|\, 2^{t}, \qquad
  V_{\mathcal{A}}(f) = |c| \big(2^{t+k} - \one\{m = t = 0\}\big).
\]
In particular, for an orthant ($t = 0$) $V_{\mathcal{S}}(f) = |c|$ for
every orientation, while $V_{\mathcal{A}}(f)$ ranges from $|c|$ (upper
orthant) to $|c|(2^{|s|}-1)$ (lower orthant).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Anchored value.} Write $\one\{x_j \le b\} = 1 - h_+(x_j - b')$ where
$b' = b^+$ is the next grid point above $b$, and
$\one\{a \le x_j \le b\} = h_+(x_j - a) - h_+(x_j - b')$. Expanding the
product over $j \in s$ gives one term per choice of a summand from each
factor: one choice for upper coordinates and two for lower and two-sided
coordinates, so $2^{t+k}$ terms, each $\pm c$ times a distinct anchored
feature or, exactly when every factor contributes its constant $1$, the
constant. The constant is available only if $m = t = 0$. By uniqueness of
the anchored representation, $V_{\mathcal{A}}(f)$ is the sum of
$|{\pm c}|$ over the non-constant terms.

\emph{Symmetric upper bound.} Use the oriented indicator directly for each
one-sided coordinate and $h_+(x_j - a_j) - h_+(x_j - b_j')$ for each
two-sided one: $2^t$ features with coefficients $\pm c$.

\emph{Lower bound.} Fix the coordinates outside $s$ at arbitrary grid values. On the resulting $|s|$-dimensional slice consider the grid
generated by the finite endpoints of $B$ and points just outside them, and
the Vitali variation (sum over grid cells of the absolute $|s|$-th mixed
difference). For $f = c\,\one_B$ it equals $|c|$ times the number of
finite corners of $B$ in $s$, which is $2^t$. Every feature
$\phi_{s',u,o}$ restricted to the slice either does not depend on some
coordinate in $s$ (if $s \not\subseteq s'$), so all its mixed differences
vanish, or is $0/1$ times a product of $|s|$ monotone one-jump indicators,
whose Vitali variation is at most $1$. The Vitali variation is a seminorm
that vanishes on constants, so for any representation
$2^t |c| \le \sum_\phi |\beta_\phi|$. This proves
$V_{\mathcal{S}}(f) \ge 2^t |c|$ and, as $V_{\mathcal{A}} \ge V_{\mathcal{S}}$,
the anchored lower bound in the case $k = 0$; for $k>0$ the anchored value
was already exact.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Mixing orientations versus selecting an anchor}

On a full finite product grid, work modulo constants and let $V_r$ denote
the anchored penalty at corner $r\in\{+,-\}^d$. Then
\begin{proposition}[Invariant convex penalty]\label{prop:convex}
The symmetric penalty satisfies
\[
 V_{\mathcal S}(f)=\inf_{\sum_r f_r=f}\sum_r V_r(f_r).
\]
Its unit ball is the convex hull of the union of the anchored unit balls.
It is the largest convex, positively homogeneous, reflection-invariant
penalty dominated by the original anchored penalty. Here reflection means
reversing the coordinate-grid order, which permutes the corners.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each oriented atom belongs to at least one global corner dictionary.
Assign each atom in a symmetric representation to one such corner; this
produces a decomposition whose summed anchored penalties are no larger
than its coefficient norm. Conversely, combining anchored representations
produces a symmetric representation at the summed cost. Taking infima
gives the equality and the unit-ball description. If $P$ is convex,
positively homogeneous, reflection invariant, and $P\le V_{+}$, then
$P\le V_r$ for every corner. Hence $P(f)\le\sum_r V_r(f_r)$ for every
decomposition, and $P(f)\le V_{\mathcal S}(f)$.
\end{proof}

The identity is an instance of the standard atomic-gauge construction for
unions of atom sets \citep{vinyes2016}. Mixed-direction rules also appear
in lasso rule ensembles \citep{friedman2008}. Here the statistical
distinction is that
mixing orientations can be cheaper than selecting any one global anchor.
On $G=\{0,1,2,3\}^2$, let
\[
 f(x)=\one\{x_1\le1,x_2\le1\}+\one\{x_1\ge3,x_2\ge3\}.
\]
Two symmetric atoms give cost two, and the full-grid mixed variation is
two, proving $V_{\mathcal S}(f)=2$. At either equal-sign corner, the
anchored expansions have costs three and one; at either mixed-sign corner,
they have costs two and two. The terms are distinct, so uniqueness of the
reduced anchored expansion gives $V_r(f)=4$ at every corner. Thus
$V_{\mathcal S}(f)<\min_r V_r(f)$. This is a representation-cost statement,
not a prediction-risk comparison with anchor selection or averaging.

Risk bounds for separate idealized estimators appear in
Appendix~\ref{sec:rates}.

\section{Computation}\label{sec:comp}

The implementation fits Gaussian or binomial zero-order HAL with main
effects and pairwise interactions. With $q$ distinct knots per
coordinate, the symmetric dictionary has
$2dq+4\binom{d}{2}q^2$ features, compared with
$dq+\binom{d}{2}q^2$ in the anchored dictionary. No column is discarded
merely because it is constant or duplicates another column on the training
sample. The default and study knots are type-7 sample quantiles at probabilities
$k/(q+1)$, $k=1,\ldots,q$, which commute with reflection in exact arithmetic.
Both orientations include equality at a knot, so discrete covariates are
covered by the same transformation argument.
The observed-rank option instead uses the construction in
Corollary~\ref{cor:ordinal}. Deterministic Gaussian and binomial tests check
its validation predictions, selected penalties, and held-out predictions
under nonlinear increasing and decreasing recodings. The empirical
prediction and causal studies evaluate the quantile default, not this option.

The primary objective uses an unpenalized intercept and unstandardized
features. Gaussian loss is the mean squared residual divided by two;
binomial loss is the mean negative log likelihood. Binomial primary fits
follow a decreasing penalty path to
the requested endpoint; intermediate penalties are computational warm starts
and do not enter validation losses. The secondary quadratic
program fixes the primary fitted linear predictors and minimizes coefficient
norm on the lasso face. A full-dictionary certificate checks that omitted
coordinates cannot improve the secondary solution. The implementation
rejects failed convergence or optimality checks instead of silently
returning an arbitrary primary solution.

Cross-validation accepts an explicit positive penalty grid and explicit
fold assignments. Each training fold constructs its own knot grid. Loss
is averaged over all validation observations; among candidates within the
specified loss tolerance of the minimum, the largest penalty is selected.
Every binomial training fold must contain both outcomes. Reusing folds and
penalties across codings is necessary for the equivariance comparison.

The dense secondary problem is intended for small and moderate dictionaries.
The implementation does not provide first-order splines, screening, or a
scalable sparse quadratic solver. Optimality tolerances are absolute, so
numerically difficult fits can fail even when a mathematical solution
exists. Such failures are part of the feasibility assessment for larger
experiments.

\section{Prediction risk on common simulation draws}\label{sec:prediction}

The prediction study gives mixed evidence about the value of the symmetric
penalty. In the fixed-knot comparison with conflicting orientations, the
ratio of symmetric mean risk to selected-anchor mean risk is
$\PilotSelectedSmallRatio$ at $n=100$, with an approximate 95\% Monte Carlo
interval $[\PilotSelectedSmallLower,\PilotSelectedSmallUpper]$, and
$\PilotSelectedLargeRatio$ at $n=300$, with interval
$[\PilotSelectedLargeLower,\PilotSelectedLargeUpper]$.
The direction changes with sample size, and both intervals include equal
risk. The exact representation-cost advantage therefore does not translate
into a stable prediction advantage in this comparison.

% Design constants and truth functions are specified in R/pilot.R and
% R/pilot_run.R; estimates are generated by R/pilot_collect.R.
The study uses \PilotReplicates{} draws in each of \PilotScenarios{}
scenarios, totaling \PilotCases{} evaluations. The fixed-knot cases reuse
the corresponding quantile-knot training samples and folds. Seven signals include
upper, lower, and mixed orthants; an interior box; two boxes with conflicting
orientations; additive steps; and a smooth signal. Their definitions are
given in Appendix~\ref{sec:prediction-design}. The main grid crosses
dimensions two and three, sample sizes $100$ and $300$, signal-to-noise
ratios $0.5$ and $2$, and three covariate distributions. These are independent
uniform coordinates; uniform margins from a Gaussian copula with latent
pairwise correlation $0.5$; and five equally likely discrete levels. In the
three-dimensional discrete case the third coordinate copies the second,
creating coincident training columns. The signal depends only on the first
two coordinates. Gaussian noise variance equals the signal variance divided
by the specified signal-to-noise ratio.
Signal variance is calculated exactly for independent and discrete
covariates and estimated from a separate $200{,}000$-point calibration
sample for correlated covariates.

All HAL variants use degree-at-most-two indicators, three quantile knots
per coordinate, five common validation folds, and penalty candidates
$0.003,0.01,0.03,0.1,0.3$. Outcomes are centered and scaled using the full
training sample for all HAL methods. Each validation training fold constructs
its own knots. Anchor selection jointly chooses the corner and penalty by
validation loss; ties favor the larger penalty and then the first corner
in a fixed enumeration. Anchor averaging chooses a common penalty from
the validation loss of the averaged predictions. Canonical orientation
uses the fold-local procedure described above. All constituent HAL fits
apply the same minimum-norm solution selection.

Risk is integrated squared prediction error against the noiseless signal.
For independent uniform and discrete covariates it is evaluated exactly;
for correlated covariates it uses a fixed independent sample of $10{,}000$
test points shared by methods and training replicates. Monte Carlo
intervals describe variation over training draws, conditional on that test
sample when numerical integration is used. They apply a paired delta method
on the log ratio of mean risks and a $t_{19}$ multiplier. They are
pointwise exploratory intervals, with no adjustment across scenarios.

The fixed-knot comparison holds the cut points at $0.25,0.5,0.75$, uses
independent uniform covariates in two dimensions, and sets the
signal-to-noise ratio to two. The anchored and symmetric dictionaries then
span the same functions almost everywhere under the covariate distribution.
Table~\ref{tab:pilot-fixed} reports absolute risks. Against canonical
orientation, symmetric risk ratios are $\PilotCanonicalSmallRatio$ and
$\PilotCanonicalLargeRatio$ at the two sample sizes. Against anchor

For example, on support $\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$ with knots

has rank $25$. Changing the global anchor changes which cells are merged.
Thus these gains cannot be attributed solely to a cheaper representation

span, but separately fitting and averaging them is a different estimator
from jointly fitting the symmetric dictionary.

duplicated second and third coordinates can also expand a single global
anchor's span. That case tests both numerical handling of coincident columns
and the consequences of the available representation.

causal accuracy. Recorded fitting times include tuning and refitting under
concurrent machine load and should not be read as isolated serial benchmarks.

\section{Discussion}

Coding conventions can affect a treatment-effect estimate through nuisance
estimation. In this application, ATT targeting does not remove that
sensitivity. The prediction-level construction provides a complementary
result: a reflection-closed dictionary with minimum-norm solution selection
makes fitted functions equivariant, including away from observed covariates.
A downstream estimator that depends only on unchanged nuisance predictions,
outcomes, treatment, and common fitting settings is then also unchanged.
This implication does not require a claim of lower prediction error or
better causal accuracy.

The admissible recodings determine the construction. The ideal symmetric
fit with interpolated quantile knots respects changes of units, origins,
and signs, but can change under a nonlinear monotone recoding even when
the training design is identical. An observed-order-statistic knot rule
closed under rank reversal supplies the stronger monotone guarantee.
The current third-moment canonical orientation uses spacings and does not
share that guarantee. More broadly, a marginal orientation rule that ignores
all increasing recodings must retain both signs on distinct observations
if it also respects reversal. These distinctions arise before choosing a
population model or evaluating prediction risk.

The symmetric penalty permits components with different preferred anchors.
An exact two-box example establishes a lower representation cost than any
single global anchor. Canonical orientation, cross-validated anchor selection, and uniformly
averaged anchor predictions provide alternatives. Canonical orientation
already removes sign dependence when its ambiguous directions are averaged;
its comparison with the symmetric fit concerns accuracy and computation. An oracle choice
among individually tuned anchors can diagnose representation costs, but is
unavailable to a practitioner. The penalty gap and fixed-lattice rate
bound do not establish a prediction-risk advantage for the implemented
estimator. The prediction study likewise finds no general advantage over
the simpler alternatives, including in the fixed-knot mechanism comparison.
Discrete-covariate gains can additionally reflect differences in dictionary
span. Computational cost and performance on the target problem remain
necessary criteria for choosing among invariant procedures.

The observational application compares a specified first-order nuisance
learner with and without canonical preprocessing. The wrapper removes the
observed coding movement but retains convergence warnings. The zero-order
equivariance theorem is a separate construction and does not apply directly
to the package's spline basis. In the known-effect pilot, both canonical
procedures agree exactly across the paired sign codings, while symmetric
HAL agrees to numerical precision. Canonical first-order nuisances improve
pilot TMLE RMSE but increase the point RMSE of both AIPW variants; symmetric
and anchored zero-order TMLE have similar pilot RMSE. Thus causal accuracy
depends on more than coding invariance. The small exploratory study and
remaining nuisance warnings prevent a general accuracy or coverage claim.

\section{Risk bounds}\label{sec:rates}

\subsection{A finite-dictionary oracle inequality}

\begin{theorem}[Slow-rate oracle inequality]\label{thm:oracle}
Let $y_i = f^*(x_i) + \varepsilon_i$ with fixed design and independent
mean-zero $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian $\varepsilon_i$. Let $X$ be any design with
$\|X_{\cdot j}\|_2^2 / n \le 1$, let $P = I - \one\one^\top/n$ and
$\|g\|_{n,c}^2 = \|Pg\|_2^2/n$. If
$\lambda \ge 2 \|(PX)^\top \varepsilon\|_\infty / n$, then any solution of
\eqref{eq:lasso} satisfies, for every $\beta$,
\[
  \|\hat f - f^*\|_{n,c}^2 \le \|f_\beta - f^*\|_{n,c}^2 + 3\lambda\|\beta\|_1 .
\]
The condition on $\lambda$ holds with probability at least $1-\delta$ for
$\lambda = 2\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2p/\delta)/n}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Profiling out $\beta_0$ turns \eqref{eq:lasso} into the lasso on $(Py, PX)$.
Comparing the objective at $\hat\beta$ and at $\beta$ and substituting
$Py = Pf^* + P\varepsilon$,
\[
  \tfrac{1}{2n}\|P(f^* - X\hat\beta)\|^2 \le
  \tfrac{1}{2n}\|P(f^* - X\beta)\|^2
  + \tfrac{1}{n}\varepsilon^\top PX(\hat\beta - \beta)
  + \lambda\|\beta\|_1 - \lambda\|\hat\beta\|_1 .
\]
The middle term is at most
$\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(\|\hat\beta\|_1 + \|\beta\|_1)$ by H\"older's inequality
and the condition on $\lambda$. Rearranging and multiplying by $2$ gives the
claim. For the probability, $(PX_{\cdot j})^\top\varepsilon / n$ is
sub-Gaussian with variance proxy $\sigma^2 \|PX_{\cdot j}\|^2/n^2 \le
\sigma^2/n$; a union bound over $p$ columns finishes.
\end{proof}

Indicator columns satisfy the normalization. With $D$ the maximal degree and
$q$ knots per coordinate, $p_{\mathcal{A}} = \sum_{r \le D}\binom{d}{r}q^r$
and $p_{\mathcal{S}} \le 2^D p_{\mathcal{A}}$, so the two choices of
$\lambda$ differ by at most a factor
$\sqrt{1 + D\log 2 / \log(2p_{\mathcal{A}}/\delta)}$.

\begin{corollary}[Misoriented boxes]\label{cor:orient}
Suppose the design points lie in a finite product grid, both dictionaries
contain all its grid knots and all sections up to degree $D$, and
$f^*=c\,\one_B$ for a nonconstant, nonempty orthant satisfying the
conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:box}, with $|s|\le D$. Under the
penalty conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:oracle}, an orthant with $k$ lower
coordinates ($t=0$, $m\ge1$) has centered empirical-error bounds
$3\lambda_{\mathcal{A}} 2^k |c|$ for HAL and $3\lambda_{\mathcal{S}} |c|$ for
symmetric HAL. For the lower orthant ($m=0$) the HAL bound is
$3\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}(2^{|s|}-1)|c|$, while the symmetric bound remains
$3\lambda_{\mathcal{S}}|c|$.
For a thinned knot set, the approximation term in
Theorem~\ref{thm:oracle} must be retained unless the indicated box
representation is still available.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}
These are upper bounds; the corollary does not show that HAL's risk is
larger. Whether the penalty gap yields a practically useful risk reduction
is an empirical question.
\end{remark}

\subsection{A lattice rate}

Fix $d$, use the full product lattice
$\{(i_1/n_1,\ldots,i_d/n_d):1\le i_j\le n_j\}$ with
$n=\prod_j n_j$, all grid knots, and $D=d$. Assume independent
$N(0,\sigma^2)$ errors with $\sigma>0$. For a fixed radius $V$, let
$\hat f_{\mathcal D,V}$ minimize squared error subject to
$V_{\mathcal D}(f)\le V$, with a free constant. For $V>0$ and
$V_{\mathcal A}(f^*)\le V$, \citet[Theorem~4.5]{fang2021} prove
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fgs}
 \frac1n\mathbb E\sum_i(\hat f_{\mathcal A,V}(x_i)-f^*(x_i))^2
 \le C_d\left(\frac{\sigma^2V}{n}\right)^{2/3}
 \left[\log\left(2+\frac{V\sqrt n}{\sigma}\right)\right]^{(2d-1)/3}
 +C_d\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.
\end{equation}
Numbering refers to arXiv:1903.01395v3.

\begin{proposition}[Symmetric rate]\label{prop:rate}
Under the preceding assumptions, if $V\ge V_{\mathcal S}(f^*)$,
$\hat f_{\mathcal S,V}$ satisfies the bound in \eqref{eq:fgs} with a
possibly different constant $C'_d$. If also $d\ge2$, $V>0$,
$n_j\ge c_s n^{1/d}$ for some $c_s\in(0,1]$, and $n$ is sufficiently
large depending on $d$, $\sigma^2/V^2$, and $c_s$, the minimax lower bound
of \citet[Theorem~4.6]{fang2021} transfers to the symmetric class. The rate
is therefore minimax optimal up to logarithmic factors under those conditions.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Expanding an oriented atom of degree at most $D$ in the reduced anchored
dictionary gives total nonconstant coefficient norm at most $2^D-1$.
A lower indicator uses its successor grid knot; constants and empty atoms
are removed first. Thus, with $c_d=2^d-1$,
\[
 V_{\mathcal S}\le V_{\mathcal A}\le c_d V_{\mathcal S}.
\]
Write $C_{\mathcal D}(V)$ for the corresponding set of lattice value
vectors and $C_{\mathcal D}(V,t)=C_{\mathcal D}(V)\cap B_2(0,t)$.
These sets are closed and convex. If $\theta^*$ belongs to
$K=C_{\mathcal S}(V)$, the triangle inequality gives
\[
 (K-\theta^*)\cap B_2(0,t)
 \subseteq C_{\mathcal S}(2V,t)
 \subseteq C_{\mathcal A}(2c_dV,t).
\]
This bounds the localized Gaussian width of $K$ by the quantity used in
\citet[Section~8.4]{fang2021}, with radius $V$ replaced by $c_dV$.
Their Lemma~8.6 states
\begin{align*}
 \log N(\epsilon,C_{\mathcal A}(V,t))
 &\le C_d\left(\frac{V\sqrt n}{\epsilon}+1\right)
 \left[\log\left(\frac{2V\sqrt n}{\epsilon}+1\right)\right]^{d-1/2}\\
 &\quad+\log\left(2+2\frac{t+V\sqrt n}{\epsilon}\right).
\end{align*}
Applying their entropy integration and projection-risk theorem (Theorem~8.1)
to the localized width gives \eqref{eq:fgs} with $c_dV$ in place of $V$.
For fixed $d$, absorb $c_d$ and the resulting change inside the logarithm
into $C'_d$. This argument transfers a width bound; it does not infer an
ordering of estimator risks from inclusion of constraint sets. If $V=0$,
the truth and estimate are constant on the lattice and the risk is
$\sigma^2/n$.

For the lower bound, $C_{\mathcal A}(V)\subseteq C_{\mathcal S}(V)$,
so a worst-case risk lower bound on the former applies to the latter.
The balanced-lattice and sample-size conditions above are those of
\citet[Theorem~4.6]{fang2021}. Their lower bound has logarithmic exponent
$2(d-1)/3$, strengthened to one for $d=2$.
\end{proof}

This result concerns a fixed-radius constrained estimator on a full lattice.
It does not establish rates for the cross-validated fixed-knot implementation
or for first-order HAL. Constants depend on dimension;
the result is not a guarantee for increasing $d$. The box costs affect the
variation-dependent terms in oracle bounds, but differing constants and
logarithms prevent a numerical comparison of the complete bounds. No risk
dominance follows. The near-parametric adaptation results for anchored
upper-right rectangles in \citet{fang2021} are not proved here for the
symmetric estimator, whose tangent cones differ.
